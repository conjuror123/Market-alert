Tremor is a bot that watches 61 markets — index funds, government bonds, currencies, gold, oil, crypto — and sends a message when one of them does something unusual. Not “moved more than 2%”, which is a dull afternoon in Solana and a once-in-years event in short-dated Treasuries, but unusual for that instrument, judged against its own history.

This page is the backtest: every message it would have sent since 2003, and how often it would have been right.

How it decides

One question, asked of every instrument, every hour

Has this just moved more than it normally does — and more than it has in a long time? Everything else follows from how that is measured.

Its own yardstick

Each instrument is ranked against its own past hours, never a shared percentage. Short Treasuries move 0.024% in a usual hour and Dogecoin 0.40% — one threshold would either drown you in crypto or never mention a bond. A move is rated by how far back you would have to go to find a bigger one in that same instrument.

Four ratings

How big the move is in that instrument's own units, on a ladder set per sector. major and extreme interrupt you; the other two are collected quietly. How often each one actually fired, for one instrument, over the whole archive:

Two kinds of message

A push is its own message and arrives the hour the move is found. That is the interruption, and there are about 56 a year. Everything milder becomes a line in a running note posted twice a week, edited in place as things happen, which never notifies.

And two ways to be unusual

Every hour is asked twice: was the move big in the instrument's own terms, and was it still big after subtracting what its whole sector did? The second catches a fund that broke ranks with its market on an otherwise ordinary day, which is often the more interesting of the two. When twenty members of one complex all move together that is one observation, and it is reported once rather than twenty times.

What a message looks like

Four real ones

Not mock-ups. These are rendered by the same code that sends them, from real rows in the archive. Each one says what moved, how far, how unusual that is for that instrument, and what it is still waiting to find out.

Does it work

Two questions worth asking of any alert

Does it catch what you would obviously want to hear about, and does it stay quiet the rest of the time? Both are measured against each instrument's own ranked history rather than a number somebody picked, so the system is not marking its own homework.

The hours you would not want to miss

The biggest 0.01% of hours each instrument has ever had — 216 of them across 23 years. How many produced a message of any kind? Crypto is left out of this headline: one coin contributes thousands of 2% hours and would flatter the result.

The hours that were nothing at all

Every hour in which an instrument moved less than it usually does — 1.17 million of them. How many produced a message? This one wants to be zero.

The once-a-day rule holds exactly

An instrument may open one event per trading day and no more — end of session for the funds, end of the UTC day for crypto. Across asset-days in the archive, the number carrying more than one event is . Nothing is folded or suppressed after the fact; the automaton simply will not open a second.

Do the moves stick

Was it news, or somebody's large order?

A move given straight back was liquidity, not information. So every alert is checked afterwards — at that day's close and the next — and the answer is written back into the message that already went out. Nothing is held while it waits: a once-in-three-years move that arrives six hours late is worth less than one that arrives now and is corrected.

The data

Free feeds, checked against each other

All of this runs on free market-data tiers, so the prices are verified rather than trusted. Each instrument is served by whichever feed was shown to price it the same way, hour by hour, against bars already stored — because a feed that disagrees by enough is an alert for a move that never happened.

Disagreement with the stored bars, in basis points

Median difference on the hourly close over the same 62 hours. One sigma of an hourly move is 20–40 bps for these instruments, so 2 bps is under a tenth of a sigma and invisible; 10 bps starts to move the ladder on its own.

Why the thin funds did not move

One provider serves a single exchange holding about 5.5% of consolidated volume. On liquid funds that is the same price to a fifth of a basis point. On thin single-commodity funds it is a different price — and a different price is an alert for a move that never happened, which is the one failure worth paying to avoid. Those fifteen stayed on a consolidated feed.

The archive provider remained configured and still held the history: neither fast feed can reach back — one serves 55 days of half-hourly bars, the other caps a response at 10,000 rows. The identity of an instrument never moved with its feed, so no stored bar, no recorded verdict and no exported event was orphaned by the change.

Reliability

It runs once an hour, for free, on GitHub's shared runners — so the question worth asking is how often it simply fails to run at all. Earlier this month one hourly run in seven was being killed on a timeout and delivering nothing.

Runs lost, by day

Share of the day's twenty-four hourly slots that carried no successful run — whether it failed, was killed on the twenty-minute timeout, or never started at all. That last one is what the six days at 100% were: the schedule had quietly stopped, so there were no failed runs to count at all.

Limits

What it can't do

Every other number on this page is measured. These are not — they are the honest list of what was still open when it was written, and the part most likely to have changed since.

It was reading five minutes and calling it an hour

Found and fixed this week, and worth telling on a page like this. The job runs five minutes past each hour and was scoring the hour it stood in — a fraction of the trading that would eventually happen — then never looking again once the hour closed. It missed the Fed's statement on 16 September: thirteen instruments moved together and none reached the phone. Every figure here is a fresh pass over complete hours, so it reflects the fix.

Some histories are short

Thirteen instruments only reach back to 2020, and one to 2022, because the free data tier stops there. A short history means a quiet instrument: the system will not rate a move it has no yardstick for, so those thirteen say less than the rest.

One feed is undocumented

The consolidated feed serving fifteen thin funds is an unversioned endpoint with no support channel. It can change shape without notice. That is why the basket is split across two providers rather than sent to one.

The feed comparison is one week long

Sixty-two overlapping hours decided which provider serves which instrument. A more volatile week might have read differently, and the comparison has not been repeated since.

It does not predict

And does not claim to. Every number here is about a move that already happened; the tier says how unusual it was, not what comes next.

Retention is a note, not a gate

A move that fully reverts still interrupted you. of pushes had given everything back by the next close — the message says so afterwards, which is all it can do.
